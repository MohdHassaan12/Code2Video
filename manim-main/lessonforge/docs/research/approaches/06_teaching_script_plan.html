From a correct script to one that teaches

The scripts lessonforge writes today are correct and tidy, and dull. The cause sits before the writer: it is told the topic's name and a general lesson shape, and nothing about how that topic is taught. This plan adds two things before the writer (sources for the topic, turned into a one-page teaching card, and a script skill that holds the craft) and one thing after it (a task the learner does by hand). The rest of the system stays as it is.

Why the script is dull

I read the two latest scripts sentence by sentence. These findings are from Square Numbers Through Prime Factorization (Class 8, Hinglish, job 20261008-190847-5038ab). The gradient descent script has the same shape.

The rule is announced, never earned.
Isliye exact test bahut simple hai. Prime factors ko identical pairs mein group kijiye.

No sentence in the lesson says why pairs make a square. The reason is short: in n × n every prime of n comes twice, once from each n. That one sentence is also what joins the dot square of chapter 3 to the factor tiles of chapter 5. The script shows both pictures and never joins them.

The opening question is answered for the learner.

Chapter 2 asks whether 1764 ladoos can sit in equal rows and columns. Chapter 3 opens with the answer, before any working:

Haan, ladoos box mein forty-two rows aur forty-two columns banenge.
The first sentence of the lesson is a definition.
Shuru karte hain prime numbers se. Prime number woh number hai jo greater than one ho…

Nothing has yet made the learner want it. This is the "boring start" you reported.

The learner only recalls.

The questions are of the kind "inmein se kaun sa prime hai, six, seven, ya nine?". There is nothing to predict about the idea itself and nothing to do by hand.

Chapter titles are the framework's own stage names.

"GIVE IT A NAME", "SAY IT BACK". The shape of the lesson shows through because nothing topic-specific replaced it.

The cause in the system

The writer receives three things: the brief (topic, class, goal, language), one of three seven-stage frameworks, and a list of rules. The frameworks already stand on research (worked examples, Rosenshine, Mayer, Muller; see docs/research/approaches/04). The rules are mostly things not to do, each added after a bad lesson.

Everything specific to the topic is missing: the reason it is true, the picture that carries it, where learners go wrong, what they could try at home. The harness reads no sources at all. engine/sources.py exists, but only the storyboard engine uses it. A model that is given only a topic's name writes the average of everything it has read about that topic.

More rules will not fix this. A script made by avoiding faults is a script with no faults and no life. The writer needs better material to write from.

The change in one picture

Goals

Four high-level goals. Under each, what can be checked on any one lesson.

1. The learner can say why

After watching, they can explain the reason, and can do more than repeat the steps.

  • The first 20 seconds hold a question the learner cannot answer yet. It is answered by name before the end.
  • The reason is one sentence on the card, and one beat of the script says it with a picture that shows it.
  • No chapter opens with a definition. Chapter titles come from the content.

2. Every lesson stands on sources

Named books, papers and the curriculum for that topic, and less of the model's average.

  • At least three sources of three kinds per lesson. Every line of the card points to a numbered passage.
  • Nothing in the script contradicts the sources. The maths is still checked by code, as now.

3. The learner does something

One task by hand, one question about it. Classrooms rarely have this.

  • One task: things found at home, five steps or fewer, a prediction first, a question after, the expected result.
  • A second version of the same task for a teacher with a full class and five minutes.
  • At least one predict question whose wrong choices are the mistakes the sources document.

4. The picture carries the idea

It shows what a teacher can only say in words.

  • Every sentence has a screen action. No beat is text only.
  • One picture per lesson is named on the card, and it changes as the idea grows.

The real test: on three pilot topics you read the old and the new script without knowing which is which and prefer the new one, and you watch the new video to the end. Counts improved last time and you still rejected the videos, so counts are only the first gate.

Seven principles, each standing on someone's work

This is the whole framework. A teacher who "writes, explains, elaborates and uses visuals" is doing numbers 3 and 4.

Principle
What it means in the script
Whose work it stands on
1. Ask before telling
Open with a question the learner cannot answer yet. The rule comes after they have guessed.
Loewenstein 1994 (curiosity is a gap you can feel); Schwartz & Bransford 1998 (telling works after a problem); Carpenter & Toftness 2017 (questions before a video).
2. One idea, one picture, an example simpler than the idea
Already your rule ("no cricket, no festival"). The card now names the one picture.
Kaminski, Sloutsky & Heckler 2008 (rich examples hurt transfer); Fyfe et al. 2014 (start concrete, then fade); Harp & Mayer 1998 and Sundararajan & Adesope 2020 (interesting extras cost learning).
3. Draw while saying
Each sentence has a screen action. Nothing arrives finished, the way a slide does.
Fiorella & Mayer 2016 (watching a diagram being drawn beats seeing it drawn already); Mayer, Fiorella & Stull 2020; Tversky et al. 2002 (animation helps only when it matches the idea); Richter et al. 2016 (pointing at the part being discussed).
4. Say why, as well as how
Each step runs in three moves. Write: it appears as it is said. Explain: why this step is allowed. Elaborate: one link, a contrast case or "what if".
Kulgemeyer 2018, 2020 (what makes an explanation and an explainer video work); Wittwer & Renkl 2008 (why explanations often fail); Rosenshine 2012 (small steps, think aloud).
5. Meet the wrong idea in the open
Already a stage. Today the mistake comes only from the person who asks for the video. It will come from sources.
Muller et al. 2008 (videos that state and break the misconception teach more); Posner et al. 1982.
6. The learner does something
Predict, try by hand, answer. The task is short and has a question, so hands and head both work.
Chi & Wylie 2014 (doing and producing beat watching); Crouch et al. 2004 (a demo teaches only if you predict first); Abrahams & Millar 2008 (hands-on without a thinking question teaches little); Hörnlein & Kulgemeyer 2026 (a video alone leaves a false feeling of understanding; a hard task after it cut that, d = 0.69).
7. Teach the topic the way that topic is taught
Every topic has its own best route, its own picture and its own traps. A good teacher knows them. The card writes them down.
Shulman 1986 (pedagogical content knowledge); Loughran, Mulhall & Berry 2004 (the CoRe, a one-page form for exactly this); Ball, Thames & Phelps 2008.

New part 1: the teaching card

This is the "Google LM" part. I read that as NotebookLM, the shape you asked for in August. Three Google systems are worth copying from, and one thing from each is enough:

  • NotebookLM answers only from the sources you load. We take: sources first, each passage numbered so a claim can point to it.
  • LearnLM is given its pedagogy as written instructions and is judged on five principles (active learning, cognitive load, metacognition, curiosity, adapting to the learner). We take: the teaching craft lives in a file a person can read, which is the skill below.
  • Learn Your Way starts from a textbook chapter, re-levels it and builds several forms with questions inside. In Google's trial with 60 students, recall after 3 to 5 days was 78% against 67% for a plain reader. We take: the textbook chapter is always the first source.

NotebookLM itself cannot be our engine. Its API is in alpha and covers notebooks, sources and audio; I found no endpoint for a script or a video (this is from third-party client docs, not Google's own). So we build its shape and use the product by hand as the lesson to beat.

What the card holds

Ten lines, adapted from the CoRe form that Loughran and colleagues use with teachers. Each line cites the passages it came from. Here it is for the square numbers lesson. I wrote this one by hand to show the shape; a real card is filled from the sources.

Teaching card · square numbers by prime factorisation · class 8
big idea
A whole number is a square exactly when each of its prime factors comes an even number of times.
why it matters
You can tell whether a large number is a square without trying every size.
the why
In n × n every prime of n arrives twice, once from each n. So each pair gives one factor to the rows and one to the columns: 36 = 2·2·3·3 = (2·3) × (2·3) = 6 × 6.
the picture
Factor tiles dealt into two equal hands. Each hand multiplies to 6, and the two 6s become the sides of a 6 by 6 dot square. For 18 = 2·3·3 the hands come out as 6 and 3: a rectangle.
needs first
Prime numbers; writing a number as a product of primes.
where learners go wrong
Pairing two different primes (a 2 with a 3). Calling 18 a square because "it has a pair".
route
Question → small squares of dots → factorise 36 and 18 side by side → deal into two hands → the why → 1764 → a number that fails.
questions
"36 dots make a square. 18 do not. Before factorising: which of 12, 16, 18 will?"
try it yourself
36 beans, or dots on squared paper. Lay out 12, 16, 18 and 36 in equal rows. Predict first which close into a square. Then write each as primes. Question: 18 has a pair of 3s, so why is it not a square? Expected: 16 and 36 close; in 18 the 2 has no partner. In class: pairs of students, squared paper, five minutes, the four factorisations collected on the board in two columns.
leave out
Square roots by long division; perfect cubes.

Compare this card with the script in the first section. The line called "the why" and the line called "the picture" are exactly what the script lacked.

How it is built. Code gathers the sources and numbers the passages (this exists). One long-context model fills the card and cites passages. Code then checks what code can check: every line has a citation, the task uses only allowed materials (paper, pencil, coins, beans, thread, a ruler, a glass of water, a torch; nothing that burns or cuts for young classes), the steps are five or fewer. The card is shown in the app before the script is written, and can be edited.

New part 2: the script skill

Today the teaching craft is spread through one long prompt in harness/writer.py, mixed with format rules. The skill is a small folder that holds the craft in one place, in words a teacher could read and correct. Code loads it into the writer and the reviewer. Format rules that code checks stay in code.

harness/skills/teaching_script/
  SKILL.md           the seven principles; the order of work; what to hand back
  card.schema.json   the ten lines of the teaching card
  moves.md           write → explain → elaborate, each with one good and one dull example
  practicum.md       how to design the try-it task; allowed materials; the class version
  checks.md          the yes/no questions the reviewer asks
  examples/          two or three scripts you have approved, one per kind of lesson

The order of work written in SKILL.md:

  1. Read the card. Say the big idea and the why in your own words before writing anything.
  2. Plan the lesson as a chain of moves, without wording: question → guess → what is seen → why → name → one worked case → your turn → try it → say it back.
  3. For each move, write the screen action first, then the sentence that is spoken over it.
  4. Read the draft as the learner. Where would they ask "but why?" Answer it there.

Two notes. The three frameworks stay, and no chapter is added: "the why" becomes a required beat inside the stage that names the idea, and the task is set in two or three sentences at the end of practice and then lives in the app. And the skill has a version that joins the cache key, as prompt versions already do, so a change to it regenerates and bills once.

Build order

Each step ends with something you can look at. If a step does not convince you, we fix it before the next one.

  1. A baseline, no code.Pick three topics. Collect their sources. Put the same sources into NotebookLM by hand and keep its video. That is the lesson to beat.
  2. The teaching card.Three cards you can read in five minutes each. Judge here first: a dull card gives a dull video, and a card takes about a minute to make, where a video has taken about ten minutes and 0.20 to 0.90 USD. What a card costs is not measured yet; this step measures it.
  3. The skill and the writer.For each topic, the old script and the new one as text, unlabelled. You choose. Nothing is rendered yet.
  4. The reviewer's teaching checks.Run on real scripts and read for what the checks themselves break, as the repetition gate once cut a definition in half.
  5. Try it yourself in the app.One full lesson through the browser, with the task card and a stored answer.
  6. The picture follows the card.The one picture carried through the lesson.

Left out on purpose, so the work does not spread: fine-tuning, personalisation by age, new figure kinds, voices. None of them makes a dull script teach.

Reading list

Curated, with what we take from each. Every paper with a link was checked against the publisher's record today.

Read these six first

  • Kulgemeyer (2020). A framework of effective science explanation videos informed by criteria for instructional explanations. Research in Science Education 50.Seven factors and fourteen features of a good explainer video, among them adapting to prior knowledge and misconceptions, avoiding digressions, and summaries. A video built to it gave more declarative knowledge (d = 0.42); for conceptual knowledge no difference was found. The closest thing to a checklist for our script.
  • Mayer, Fiorella & Stull (2020). Five ways to increase the effectiveness of instructional video. Educational Technology Research and Development 68.Draw as you explain; make the learner produce something during the video; skip attractive footage that is off the point.
  • Loughran, Mulhall & Berry (2004). In search of pedagogical content knowledge in science. Journal of Research in Science Teaching 41.The CoRe: eight questions that capture how one idea is taught. Our teaching card is this form plus the task.
  • Muller, Bewes, Sharma & Reimann (2008). Saying the wrong thing: improving learning with multimedia by including misconceptions. Journal of Computer Assisted Learning 24.A clear video can leave learners confident and wrong. Stating the wrong idea and breaking it teaches more.
  • Abrahams & Millar (2008). Does practical work really work? International Journal of Science Education 30.Practical work gets students to do things with objects and rarely to think with the ideas. So our task always carries a prediction and a question.
  • Chi & Wylie (2014). The ICAP framework. Educational Psychologist 49.Four levels of what a learner is doing: watching, handling, producing, discussing. Each step up teaches more. A measure for our questions and tasks.
