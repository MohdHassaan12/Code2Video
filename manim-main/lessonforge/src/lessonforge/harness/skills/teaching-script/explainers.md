# What the best explainers do (and the research behind it)

Read 2026-10-16 from Muller's video studies, Sanderson's own FAQ, Khan, Feynman, Mayer's
multimedia principles, Rosenshine, the worked-example and prediction studies, and what
Indian teachers who are watched by millions are known for. Each rule names its source.
They come on top of the principles above, and where one sharpens an earlier rule, follow
this one.

## The shape of the lesson

1. **Start from a situation that needs the idea, not from the idea.** The first sentence
   is something the learner can picture with a problem in it ("a ladder slips if it
   leans too low: how do we say how steep it is?"). Never "Here is a right triangle"
   and never a definition. (Sanderson: "never start with definitions"; concrete before
   abstract.)
2. **Remind them of what they already know, in one sentence, before the new step.** Name
   the earlier idea this one stands on ("you already know Pythagoras: three, four,
   five"). (Rosenshine, principle 1: review prior learning.)
3. **Say the wrong idea out loud early, then break it with the example.** Put the
   common mistake in the first third of the lesson, as the thing many students believe,
   and let the running example show it failing. It comes back as a wrong choice in the
   last question. A clear lesson that never meets the mistake feels easy and teaches
   almost nothing. (Muller 2008: refutation videos d = 0.79 against plain exposition;
   students rated the plain one "clear" and their scores did not move.)
4. **Ask for a prediction before every reveal that matters.** "What do you think
   happens to the ratio when we double both sides?", then a pause, then the answer.
   Watching without predicting teaches about as much as not watching. (Crouch et al.
   2004.)
5. **The formula is the NAME of something already seen.** Work the case first, notice
   the pattern together, then write the formula. (Sanderson; Rosenshine: models first.)
6. **Worked example, then one with a step left for them, then one alone.** In the second
   example stop at the key step, ask, pause, then go on. (Sweller and Cooper; Renkl's
   fading; Paas: completion problems.)
7. **Small steps: one new idea per chapter, at most two new words per beat.** A beat that
   defines three terms is a glossary, not an explanation. (Rosenshine 2; Mayer
   segmenting d = 0.70.)
8. **Answer the opening question by name near the end, and end on one question they
   answer before you do.** (Rosenshine 3 and 6; retrieval practice.)

## Inside each beat

9. **Explain ONE thing per beat, fully, rather than four things in a list.** Say what is
   happening, then WHY with "because", then what it means for our example. If a beat
   has more than one new fact, it needs to be two beats or one fact must go.
10. **A new case gets a new picture.** If the words move to other numbers or another
    object, the figure must show those numbers. Never talk about a 5-12-13 triangle over
    a 6-8-10 one. If the picture cannot change, keep the same case. (Mayer: temporal
    contiguity, d = 1.30.)
11. **Say what you are doing to the picture as you do it, like a tutor beside them:**
    "let's mark the angle here", "now I'll double this side". Use "we", "let's", "you",
    and contractions. (Khan: "someone over your shoulder"; Mayer personalization,
    d = 0.79.)
12. **The screen shows labels, numbers and working, never the sentence being spoken.**
    (Mayer redundancy, d = 0.87.)
13. **Signal the important moment out loud:** "here's the key part", "so this is why".
    Once or twice a lesson, not every beat. (Mayer signaling, d = 0.46.)
14. **An analogy says where it stops working.** "It's like a ramp, but unlike a ramp…"
    (Feynman: he refused an analogy that hid the real mechanism.)
15. **Cut anything that does not answer the opening question.** No fun facts, no second
    story. (Mayer coherence, d = 0.70; Muller's "extended exposition" with interesting
    extras did no better than the plain one.)

## For Indian school learners

16. **Everyday Indian things at small numbers:** a ladder against a house wall, a
    school bag strap, rupees. No story around them, and never a sports ground or a
    festival.
17. **One sentence near the end on how this shows up in the NCERT exercise or board
    question,** for example: "in the exam they'll give you two sides and ask for all six
    ratios: that's exactly what we just did". Never as the opening.
