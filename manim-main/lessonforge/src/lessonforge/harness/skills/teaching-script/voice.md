# How the teacher talks

The script is HEARD, once, at the speed of speech. Write what a good teacher says out
loud while drawing, never what a textbook prints or what a form asks. The lessons this
was read off were correct and still sounded like a form being read aloud: an order in
every second sentence, the same question asked four times, English sentences with Hindi
glue words. Each rule below names one of those faults.

## 1. Make the sentence in the language it is spoken in

For Hinglish, make the sentence in Hindi first, then say the subject's own terms in
English. Never write an English sentence and swap in Hindi joining words.

- Test: take the English terms out. What is left must still be a Hindi sentence.
- Small words stay Hindi: ek, do, yeh, woh, thoda, bahut, andar, bahar, patli, gol,
  chhota, alag. English is for the subject's terms (cell membrane, nucleus, energy,
  prime factor), for the numbers of the working and for the operations.
- "one" and "a" are never used as an article: "ek patli si boundary", never "one thin
  outer edge". "Ek" and "do" stay Hindi; from three on a count is English and takes an
  English noun ("four jobs", never "char jobs" and never "four baatein").

| Translated (wrong) | Spoken (right) |
|---|---|
| Sabse chhote picture mein one thin outer edge aur one soft inside space hai. | Bahar ek patli si boundary hai, aur andar yeh naram, jelly jaisa hissa. |
| Boundary ko contents saath rakhne hain, lekin selected materials ko cross bhi karne hain. | Is boundary ke do kaam hain: andar ki cheezein andar rahein, aur zaroorat ki cheezein aa-ja sakein. |
| Controls what enters and leaves ko kis part se connect karenge? | Kya andar aayega aur kya bahar jaayega, yeh kaun sa part tay karta hai? |

For English, write as you would say it to one learner across a table: "let's", "we",
"you", short forms ("that's", "it's"). Never "Observe the diagram", "It can be seen
that", "Consider the following".

## 2. Talk with the learner, do not give orders

"Dekhiye", "sochiye", "kijiye", "pehchaniye" are orders. One in a beat is a teacher; one
in every sentence is a drill. Most sentences simply say what is happening, or do it
together: "ab hum inhe do hisson mein baantte hain", "chaliye, isi ko eighteen par
aazmate hain", "yahan kya hua?".

- At most ONE order in a beat. Only the beat that sets the try-it task may have more:
  it is a list of things to do.

## 3. Point at the thing, never at the screen

The learner is already looking at it. Say "yeh", "yahan", "is taraf", "yeh gol sa
part". Never say "picture mein", "screen par", "diagram mein", "side by side pictures",
"label ke saath", "is video mein", and never describe where things sit on the screen.
Say what the thing is and what it does. And never speak of a mark that is not there:
if the picture has no arrow from a job to a part, do not say "the arrow goes to the
cell membrane"; say what the part does.

## 4. The name comes after the thing

Show it, say what it does, then give its name: "Yeh patli si boundary cell ko ghere
hue hai, aur yahi tay karti hai ki kya andar aayega. Isi ko cell membrane kehte hain."
Never open with a row of definitions ("X ek … hai. Y ek … hai. Z woh … hai."). What the
learner needs first is told as one joined thought.

## 5. Join the sentences

Speech is held together by small words: toh, isliye, kyunki, lekin, matlab, yaani, aur
phir (so, because, but, which means, and then). A sentence starts from what the one
before it ended on. Three sentences in a row with no joining word are a list, and a list
is not an explanation.

- A beat's first sentence picks up the last thought of the beat before it. It does not
  start afresh with "Ab …" every time.

## 6. Change the rhythm

Follow a long sentence with a short one. After an explanation, three words land: "Bas,
itna hi." "Yahi reason hai." No two sentences next to each other open with the same
word, and no word opens the first sentence of two beats in a row.

## 7. Ask once, and mean it

- A question is asked ONE time in the lesson. Later beats work towards its answer; they
  never ask it again in other words. Only the recap may ask the opening question again,
  and then the learner is the one who answers it.
- A stop (a question with thinking time) is the INTERACTION rule's stop: never more
  than one in a chapter, never two stops about the same question.
- Any other question is the teacher thinking aloud, and it is answered in the same
  beat, so it is never the beat's last sentence: "Agar yeh boundary poori band ho jaaye
  toh? Oxygen andar aa hi nahi sakti."
- After a stop, confirm the ANSWER, not the learner: "Haan, alag parts chahiye,
  kyunki …". Never praise an answer nobody heard ("Bilkul sahi!", "Shabash!").

## 8. Small spoken words

"Achha", "dekho", "toh", "hai na?", "bas", "yaani" are how a teacher sounds. At most
one in a beat, none inside a line of working. Never a greeting, never "doston" or
"bachchon", never "is video mein hum seekhenge".

## 9. Explain like a lecturer, not like notes

The five lessons of 2026-10-19 were correct and the owner called them "very complex and
cryptic". Fifteen lectures on the same topics on YouTube showed the difference: a
lecturer asks about once every two minutes, joins nearly every sentence to the one
before, and never says the plan of the lesson out loud. Ours asked in every beat, wrote
colons, and read stage names as narration.

- No colons, no labels. "So the claim is: switching wins" is a note. Say "Here's the
  surprising part. If you switch, you win two times out of three."
- Never say the step of the lesson: "here is the claim", "test it", "change one
  thing", "start small", "your turn", "make a guess first". Do the step; do not name it.
- A beat explains. Ask only where the chapter is told it stops; any other question is
  answered in the next sentence, and most thoughts are better said than asked.
- Whole sentences, with the small words left in ("the car is equally likely to be
  behind any of the three doors", never "car equally likely, any door").

As written:

> Start small: the car is equally likely behind any door. What chance does your first
> pick hide it?

As said:

> Let's start with your first pick. The car is just as likely to be behind any of the
> three doors, so the door you chose hides it one time in three, and the two doors you
> didn't choose hide it the other two times.

## One beat, as it was written and as it is said

As written (four orders and counts in English, the screen described, a question that
was already asked):

> Ab blank cheek cell ko dhyaan se dekhiye. Sabse chhote picture mein one thin outer
> edge aur one soft inside space hai. Isi cheek cell mein one round control part aur
> two bean-shaped energy parts bhi honge. Toh sochiye, kya different jobs ke liye
> different parts chahiye honge?

As said (Hindi sentences with English terms, the thing pointed at, one thought leading
to the next, one question that moves the lesson on and is answered at once):

> Yeh hamare gaal ki ek cell hai. Bahar ek patli si boundary, aur andar yeh naram,
> jelly jaisa hissa. Abhi bas itna hi dikh raha hai. Lekin isi chhoti si cell ko
> oxygen andar leni hai, waste bahar nikalna hai, aur apne kaam ke liye energy bhi
> chahiye. Itna sab ek patli boundary akeli kar legi? Nahi kar sakti, isliye andar
> aur parts hain, aur ab hum unhe ek ek karke dhoondhenge.

The same beat in English, as written:

> Now observe the blank cheek cell. In the smallest picture there is one thin outer
> edge and one soft inside space.

and as said:

> This is one cell from the inside of your cheek. A thin edge around it, something
> soft inside, and that's all we can see so far. But this tiny thing has to take in
> oxygen, push out waste and find energy for its work. Can a thin edge do all that
> alone? It can't, so there's more inside, and we'll find it one part at a time.
